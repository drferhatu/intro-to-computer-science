## Part 1 · What is computer science?

> [!definition] Computation
> **Input → algorithm → output.** Computer science is the study of information: how to represent it (the input and output) and how to process it (the algorithm). Computational thinking is applying that lens to any problem.

Picture a box. A problem goes in on the left, a solution comes out on the right, and the box in the middle is what this whole course is about. But before we can say anything about the box, we need to agree on how the things going in and coming out are written down. We use words, numbers and pictures. A computer has only electricity, which is either flowing or not.

## Part 2 · Representation: everything is bits

### Unary, decimal, binary

How high can you count on one hand? Five, if each finger is one unit. That is **unary**. Now treat each finger as a **bit**: down is 0, up is 1. Suddenly one hand counts to 31, and two hands to 1023. That is not a trick, it is *place value*, and it is the whole secret of the machine in front of you.

In **decimal** we have ten digits, and each position is worth ten times the one to its right (1, 10, 100). A transistor is a switch, on or off, so a computer has two digits, and each position is worth *two* times the one to its right (1, 2, 4, 8, …).

### Text: ASCII and Unicode

Bits are just numbers. To store letters we need an **agreement**, and the 1963 agreement is **ASCII**: 65 is `A`, 66 is `B`, 97 is `a`, 48 is the character `0`, 32 is a space. So what does 72 73 33 say? Look it up in the table: `H`, `I`, `!`.

ASCII has 128 characters, enough for English and little else. **Unicode** extends the same idea to about 150 000 characters, every writing system and every emoji, stored as 1 to 4 bytes per character in **UTF-8**: `ş` is two bytes, `😂` (code point 128 514) is four. Every emoji you have ever sent was a number.

> [!warning] The same bits, different meanings
> 01001000 01101001 is `Hi` as text, 18 537 as a 16-bit number, and part of a dark teal pixel as color. Bits carry no meaning; **programs** do, by context. That is why a program that misreads a file shows garbage, and why every file format starts with a header saying "read me as an image".

### Color, images, sound, video

- A **pixel** is three bytes, red, green, blue, each 0–255. (255, 255, 255) is white, (0, 0, 0) black, (255, 0, 0) red. Zoom into any emoji far enough and the squares appear. "Enhance!" in crime shows is impossible: there is nothing behind the bytes.
- An **image** is a grid of pixels plus a header; a **video** is many images per second; **sound** is a wave sampled 44 100 times a second, each sample a number.
- **Programs** are bits too, instructions the processor understands. We meet them in week 3.

## Part 3 · Algorithms

> [!definition] Algorithm
> A finite, precise, step-by-step procedure for solving a problem. Precise enough for a machine that understands nothing.

The classic example: find *John Harvard* in a 1 000-page phone book.

1. **Page by page**: correct, up to 1 000 steps. Linear search.
2. **Two pages at a time**: twice as fast, but you can overshoot and need a check step. Still linear.
3. **Open the middle**, decide which half, tear the other half away, repeat: 10 steps for 1 000 pages, 20 for a million, 30 for a billion. Binary search, and it only works because the book is **sorted**.

| Pages | Linear | Binary |
|---|---|---|
| 1 000 | 1 000 | 10 |
| 1 000 000 | 1 000 000 | 20 |
| 1 000 000 000 | 1 000 000 000 | 30 |

Correctness first, then **efficiency**; the second column is why Google feels instant. Week 5 turns this into a science (Big O).

### Pseudocode

Write the algorithm in English with structure, and the vocabulary of every programming language appears by itself:

```text
1  Pick up phone book
2  Open to middle of phone book
3  Look at page
4  If person is on page
5      Call person
6  Else if person is earlier in book
7      Open to middle of left half of book
8      Go back to line 3
9  Else if person is later in book
10     Open to middle of right half of book
11     Go back to line 3
12 Else
13     Quit
```

| Idea | In the pseudocode | In Scratch (today) | In C (week 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **function** | pick up, open, look at, call | `say`, `play sound`, your own blocks | `printf`, `main`, your own functions |
| **condition** | if / else if / else | `if … then … else` | `if (…) { } else { }` |
| **boolean expression** | person is on page | `touching`, `=`, `<`, `or` | `x == 5`, `n < 10` |
| **loop** | go back to line 3 | `forever`, `repeat`, `repeat until` | `while`, `for` |
| **variable** | (the current page) | orange variable blocks | `int page = 500;` |

Keep this table in mind for the rest of the page: every Scratch program below is one or two rows of it.

## Part 4 · Scratch: the five ideas, one block at a time

Scratch is a block language from MIT. You drag blocks instead of typing, so there are no syntax errors, and we can concentrate on the ideas. Open [scratch.mit.edu](https://scratch.mit.edu) → **Create**. Three areas: **blocks** on the left, **scripts** in the middle, the **stage** on the right. Colors are categories: Motion, Looks, Sound, Events, Control, Sensing, Operators, Variables, My Blocks.

> [!demo] hello0 · one function
> [hello0.sb3](/scratch/hello0.sb3): `when green flag clicked` → `say Hello, world!`. Click the flag, and that is a whole program: an **event** (the flag) and a **function** (`say`) with an **argument** (the text). Change the text, click again. This is `printf("hello, world")` in disguise.

> [!demo] hello1 · input and a return value
> [hello1.sb3](/scratch/hello1.sb3): `ask What's your name? and wait` → `say (join hello, answer)`. Look at the shapes of the blocks. `ask` is a function with a **side effect** (a prompt on the stage) and a **return value** (`answer`, the rounded block). `join` takes two inputs and returns one string. Functions plug into functions. What happens if you `say answer` without `join`? Try it.

> [!demo] hello2 · the same idea, a different function
> [hello2.sb3](/scratch/hello2.sb3): the same program, but `speak` from the Text to Speech extension instead of `say`. Once you know the shape, event → function with inputs, a new function costs nothing to learn. This is the whole reason libraries exist.

> [!demo] meow0 → meow1 → meow2 · the loop
> [meow0.sb3](/scratch/meow0.sb3): `play sound Meow until done`. Now [meow1.sb3](/scratch/meow1.sb3): the same block three times with `wait 1 seconds` between. It works. What is wrong with it? Copy-paste: change the wait and you change it three times, and a hundred meows would need a hundred blocks. [meow2.sb3](/scratch/meow2.sb3): `repeat 3` around one `play sound` and one `wait`. Same behavior, one place to change. Change 3 to 10 and click the flag. This is the **loop** row of the table.

> [!demo] meow3 · abstraction: your own block
> [meow3.sb3](/scratch/meow3.sb3): *My Blocks → Make a Block* named `meow`; its definition is `play sound Meow`, `wait 1`. The main script is now `repeat 3 { meow }`, and it reads like English. **Abstraction**: once `meow` works, you stop thinking about its inside. Every large program is abstraction on abstraction; this is the most important idea of the day.

> [!demo] meow4 · a block with an input
> [meow4.sb3](/scratch/meow4.sb3): the block is now `meow n times`, and its definition contains `repeat n { play sound; wait 1 }`. The main script is one block: `meow 3 times`. Compare with `say`: our block takes an argument exactly like Scratch's own. We just wrote a function with a parameter. In week 3 this is `void meow(int n)`.

> [!demo] pet0 · conditions and forever
> [pet0.sb3](/scratch/pet0.sb3): `forever { if touching mouse-pointer then play sound Meow }`. Move the mouse over the cat. Two new rows of the table: **loop** again (`forever`, the "go back to line 3" of pseudocode) and **condition** with a **boolean expression** (`touching mouse-pointer` is either true or false). Why is the `forever` needed around the `if`? Because a single `if` runs once, at the moment the flag is clicked; the check has to happen again and again.

> [!demo] pet1 · events from the world
> [pet1.sb3](/scratch/pet1.sb3): `when video motion > 50 → play sound Meow` (allow the camera). No `forever` needed: this is an **event**, like the green flag or a key press. Programs react to the world; a phone app is nothing but event handlers.

> [!demo] Oscartime 1 → 6 · a game grows one idea at a time
> [oscartime-1.sb3](/scratch/oscartime-1.sb3): two sprites, Oscar and Trash; `forever { if touching Trash then … else … }`, so the trash disappears when you drag it onto the can. [2](/scratch/oscartime-2.sb3) and [3](/scratch/oscartime-3.sb3): the trash falls from a **random** x position (`pick random`). [4](/scratch/oscartime-4.sb3): the falling moves into a **custom block**, and the script reads clean. [5](/scratch/oscartime-5.sb3) and [6](/scratch/oscartime-6.sb3): a **variable** `score`, `set score to 0` at the start, `change score by 1` on every hit. Every row of the table, in one small game. The [full Oscartime](/scratch/oscartime.sb3) has the same ideas with more sprites, a countdown and broadcasts. Nothing new, just more.

> [!demo] Ivy's Hardest Game 0 → 2 · reuse
> [Harvard 0](/scratch/ivys-hardest-game-harvard-0.sb3): the player sprite moves with the arrow keys between two walls; a custom block checks `if touching left wall or touching right wall` and bounces back. [1](/scratch/ivys-hardest-game-harvard-1.sb3) adds a Yale sprite that patrols and catches you; [2](/scratch/ivys-hardest-game-harvard-2.sb3) adds MIT. The *same* custom block runs in every enemy sprite. Abstraction is what makes level 3 cheap after level 2.

> [!demo] Whack-a-Mole · a real project
> [whack-a-mole.sb3](/scratch/whack-a-mole.sb3): four gophers, `when this sprite clicked`, `broadcast`, `repeat until`, a `score`, a `count down`, a `level`. Play it for a minute and name the rows of the table as you spot them. This is the size of a Scratch project at the *advanced* end of the lab; the minimum is much smaller.

### What Scratch taught you, in one list

- **Functions** with arguments (`say hello`) and return values (`answer`, `join`).
- **Loops**: `repeat n`, `forever`, `repeat until`.
- **Conditions** on **boolean expressions**: `if touching … then … else`, `and`, `or`, `not`.
- **Variables**: `set score to 0`, `change score by 1`.
- **Events**: green flag, key pressed, sprite clicked, broadcast received.
- **Abstraction**: `make a block`, with or without inputs; reuse across sprites.

## Part 5 · Where this goes

The same program, twice:

```text
when green flag clicked
say [hello, world]
```

```c
#include <stdio.h>

int main(void)
{
    printf("hello, world\n");
}
```

`main` is the green flag, `printf` is `say`. The semicolon ends a statement; the braces group statements into a block. Next week we type this ourselves, and every block you dragged today gets a text spelling. Not in today's lab, though: today's lab is all Scratch and bits.

## Summary

- Computers hold only bits. Numbers, text, color, sound, images and programs are all bits under an agreement (binary, ASCII/Unicode, RGB).
- 8 bits = 1 byte = 256 values; n bits give 2ⁿ values.
- An algorithm is a precise procedure; binary search needs a sorted input and takes about log₂ n steps. Pseudocode is how we design before we code.
- Five ideas in every language: functions, conditions (on booleans), loops, variables, events; plus abstraction (custom blocks) to manage size.
